Für Elise — Looping Tutorial (v2, from a verified score)

Ludwig van Beethoven, Für Elise (Bagatelle in A minor, WoO 59) — public domain. We loop the famous opening strain: 16 bars of 3/8 (48 eighth-note steps) in A minor, played in live, one layer at a time, on the Behringer Kick (0700-AAM) simulator.

Source — verified, not from memory. Every pitch, duration and sustain below was extracted from the Mutopia Project's engraving of the score (Mutopia-2015/08/18-931, typeset by Stelios Samelis, public domain; file fuer_elise_mutopia_woo59.mid, LilyPond output at 384 ticks/beat, 3/8). Two independent downloads of the file agreed byte-for-byte. The recipe must pass two gates before it ships: the composition check (the produced MIDI is the composition, note for note) and the output audit (the rendered sound matches the expected pattern — notes present, sustains held, crescendo audible).

The strain, as written (step = one eighth note, 0–47)

Melody (the line everyone knows): E5 D♯5 E5 · D♯5 E5 B4 · D5 C5 A4 held 2 · (figure) · B4 held 2 · (figure) · C5 held 2 · (figure) — then the whole figure repeats — A4 held 2 · (figure) · B4 held 2 · and the close: C5 B4 A4 held 4.

In steps: melody at 0–8 (A4 at step 8, held 2), 14 (B4, held 2), 20 (C5, held 2), 24–32 (the repeat, A4 at 32), 38 (B4, held 2), 42–44 (C5, B4, A4 held 4, steps 44–47).

The opening figure plays alone. No accompaniment enters until the third bar — the oscillation is solo, exactly as written.

Bass — the roots do not land on the downbeat. They answer on the third eighth of the held-note bars: A2 at steps 8, 20, 32, 44; E2 at steps 14, 38.

Broken chords — the figure Beethoven wrote, split across both hands, climbing through each chord under the held notes: steps 9–13: E3 A3 C4 E4 A4 · steps 15–19: E3 G♯3 E4 G♯4 B4 · steps 21–23: E3 A3 E4 · steps 33–37 and 39–41 repeat the two figures · steps 45–46: E3 A3 under the final held A4.

Harmony — A minor throughout, turning to E major under the held B4s (steps 14–19 and 38–43), back to A minor for the close.

Dynamics — the source MIDI is flat-velocity (LilyPond does not encode the score's dynamics), so this layer is editorial and labelled as such: the strain is dolce, the second statement swells slightly, and the closing descent (C5–B4–A4) carries the crescendo. The output audit measures that crescendo in the rendered audio. On the pads, play it as one gesture: even pressure through the strain, lean in slightly on the repeat, and press harder over the last three notes (C5–B4–A4).

Song map — four phrases of four bars. Phrase 1 (steps 0–13): the solo oscillation, the held A4, and its broken-chord answer. Phrase 2 (steps 14–23): the held B4 over E major, the held C5, their answers. Phrase 3 (steps 24–37): the repeat — oscillation, held A4, answer. Phrase 4 (steps 38–47): the held B4 over E major, then the close C5–B4–A4 with the crescendo. Learn it a phrase at a time; the loop doesn't care where you start listening.

The steps (as performed in the recording)

  1. Tempo + drums. The strain's pulse is one eighth note of 0.30 s (≈100 BPM counted in quarter notes). On the machine you tap the beat the display counts: tap TAP TEMPO in time until the display reads 44–46 BPM — the display counts the tapped beat while the grid step carries the eighth note, so it reads about half. Anywhere in 44–46 is right; the loop is the same. Then program the drums in the step sequencer (GRID view): kick on the four beats, snare on the off-beats, hats on every other step. Checkpoint: the display reads 44–46 BPM, and the step grid shows the pattern lit in orange — kick ×4, snare ×2, hats filling every other step.
  2. Keygroup. Strike the melodic source pad, press 16 LEVEL — the pads become a keyboard spanning the tune's range. Checkpoint: 16 LEVEL is lit and the display shows BANK A (Pads 1–16) — the pads now play one sound at sixteen pitches.
  3. OVERDUB + chords. Press PLAY, light OVERDUB — every note played now is written into the loop. Play one triad per bar: A minor, turning to E under the held notes. Checkpoint: OVERDUB stays lit, the BAR counter cycles, and after the first cycle the chords come back around on their own. Good to know: the machine writes each strike into the step that is playing when the pad is hit — timing inside a step doesn't matter, but a strike that lands in the neighbouring step is written there. If a note ever appears one step off, that's why.
  4. Bass. Still overdubbing: the roots on their written steps — low A, low E — held underneath. Checkpoint: one low root per held-note bar, landing on the third eighth — you hear it answer under the chord, not on the downbeat.
  5. Broken chords. The climbing figure, low to high, exactly as written above. Checkpoint: every held-note bar now carries its climbing answer figure, and the loop holds drums, chords, bass and figures together.
  6. Melody. Switch to the lead source and play the tune over the top — figure, held notes, the repeat, and the swelling close. Checkpoint: the tune sits clearly on top in the flute voice — the held A, B and C ring their full length, and the last three notes swell.
  7. Full loop. Stop the capture; the finished strain loops. One final A-minor sonority ends the performance. Checkpoint: STOP is lit, OVERDUB is off, and the finished strain loops cleanly from bar 1.

The principles in this song

Every LoopProof video teaches looping itself, not just the song — six principles, spoken at the step that demonstrates them:

  1. Start with a pulse you can trust — step 1: every later layer leans on this beat.
  2. One layer, one job — step 3: the chords only hold the harmony; that frame is what the rest hangs on.
  3. Leave space — step 4: a loop repeats forever, so the bass plays one root in the right place instead of four nervous ones.
  4. Listen before you add — step 5: let the loop come around and play only what you hear missing.
  5. The melody goes on last and on top — step 6: a voice of its own, so the ear knows where the story is.
  6. A good loop sounds finished at any moment — step 7: every stage of this build was already music.

The full set lives in the repo's PRINCIPLES.md.
